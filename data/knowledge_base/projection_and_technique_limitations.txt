TECHNICAL ARTIFACTS AND PROJECTION LIMITATIONS IN CHEST RADIOGRAPHY:
Standard upright posteroanterior (PA) projection provides superior anatomical fidelity because the anterior chest wall directly contacts the image detector, minimizing geometric magnification of cardiac and mediastinal structures. By contrast, anteroposterior (AP) portable projections create substantial apparent cardiomegaly and mediastinal widening due to diverging X-ray beams and increased heart-to-detector distance.

Inspiratory adequacy significantly modulates radiographic appearance. A high-quality inspiratory radiograph requires visualization of at least 9 to 10 posterior ribs, or 6 anterior ribs, above the mid-point of the right hemidiaphragm. Poor inspiratory effort or expiratory technique compresses basilar bronchovascular structures, producing spurious bibasilar haziness that mimics pneumonia, atelectasis, or early pulmonary edema.

Patient rotation alters thoracic symmetry and may falsely simulate unilateral pulmonary opacification or tracheal deviation. Rotation is assessed by measuring the horizontal distance between the medial ends of the clavicles and the central line of thoracic vertebral spinous processes. Unequal distances signify rotation toward the side with the shorter gap.

Penetration technique dictates diagnostic utility. On an appropriately penetrated radiograph, the thoracic vertebral bodies and disc spaces should be faintly perceptible behind the cardiac silhouette. Underpenetrated images obscure retrocardiac disease and basilar lung markings, whereas overpenetrated images wash out subtle ground-glass infiltrates and small non-calcified nodules.
